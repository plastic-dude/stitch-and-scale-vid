# Director Research — False Completion and Human Evidence Gates

**Research date:** 2026-08-19 UTC  
**Research question:** What current knitwear workflow evidence shows that a pattern can look complete while still requiring distinct human evidence gates, and how should that change this campaign’s story?  
**Researcher:** Director/Planner  
**Decision status:** Research complete; production handoff blocked by missing current product proof.

## Finding

Current knitwear workflow sources distinguish mathematical and editorial technical editing from test knitting and final print/presentation checks. A current technical editor describes checking stitch counts, measurements, grading, schematics, charts, grammar, clarity, and layout, then sending the pattern to test knitters after the editing rounds; the editor also describes a final pass after testing.[1] A current designer workflow account describes grading and spreadsheets, sample knitting, technical editing, test knitting across body types, reformatting, print-readiness, and final review as separate stages rather than one completion checkbox.[2] The Knitting Guild Association’s current professional-designer curriculum likewise treats grading, yardage, shaping, charting, pattern writing, working with tech editors, running test knits, layout, pricing, rights, and a ready-to-publish pattern as distinct areas of practice; it also requires a knitted sample for review in its business-of-design module.[3]

The evidence supports a story about **false completion**: a checked or polished-looking pattern is not the same thing as a pattern whose math, instructions, charts, schematics, physical print, and test-knit evidence have each received the appropriate human review. This is a workflow observation, not proof that every designer follows the same sequence. The cited sources are industry practitioners and a professional guild, not a representative survey of independent designers; audience prevalence and conversion impact remain uncertain.

## Platform evidence

For a vertical derivative, YouTube currently classifies square or vertical videos up to three minutes as Shorts when uploaded after 15 October 2024.[4] Instagram’s current help documentation permits Reels between 1.91:1 and 9:16, with at least 30 FPS and 720-pixel minimum resolution.[5] TikTok’s June 2026 in-feed specification recommends 9:16 at a minimum of 540×960, permits up to 10 minutes, and states that key elements must remain inside a safe zone whose size varies with dimensions and caption length.[6] These requirements reinforce a platform-native 9:16 proof composition with protected caption and UI-safe areas; they do not justify placing captions over the proof surface.

## Decision changed by research

The nearest prior concept is `publication-second-shift`, which framed the broad invisible labor that begins after creative design feels finished. This run moves narrower and more concrete: **the danger is not “more work after finishing,” but the false closure created by a completed-looking checkbox**. The provisional angle slug is `false-completion`; it is materially different in subject, hook, visual metaphor, proof surface, emotional turn, and final line.

The proposed concept cannot be approved for generation because this repository contains no current product-proof registry, approved UI captures, or dated evidence for the specific release-gate surfaces the concept would need to show. The next action is therefore: **Product/Archive owner must add current, approved, non-private captures or a proof registry entry for one observable human-gate workflow, with labels and version/date; then Director reruns claim and asset validation before writing an approved-for-generation brief.**
