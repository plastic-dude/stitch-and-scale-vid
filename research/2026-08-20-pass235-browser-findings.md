## Fresh question

**What qualifier must travel with a craft value or label—required, optional, estimated, conditional, unknown, or not applicable—so a bare number or status is not overread as a universal instruction or completed fact?**

The question is distinct from pass 234 `editability-state`, which asks whether a named source or artifact may be changed and which review reopens. It is distinct from pass 233 `requirement-interpretation-state`, which asks what one requirement means before action; from `uncertainty-context`, `basis-label`, `confidence-state`, and `scope-of-proof-state`, which respectively concern uncertainty, source/basis, confidence, and validity scope; and from `evidence-type`, `evidence-coverage`, and `sufficiency-state`, which concern evidence affordance, coverage, and minimum evidence. Pass 235 focuses on **the qualifier attached to a single value or field** and the decision it changes.

## Evidence gathered

### 1. Craft guidance uses qualifiers to prevent a bare value from carrying too much meaning

The Craft Yarn Council’s current industry checklist requires precise yarn type, put-up, yardage and weight, fiber content, quantities for each project size, needle/hook sizes in millimeters and U.S. sizes, all materials and accessories with sizes and quantities, measurements for each size, the size made, right-side/wrong-side direction, stitch totals after shaping, finishing details, charts, and a schematic drawn to scale. It also says designers should reconfirm yarn and color selection, check dye lots, reconfirm the sample size, keep notes, and discuss problems with an editor or design director. These labels and conditions make the difference between a bare number and a usable record; they do not imply that any product automatically supplies or validates them. [1]

The CYC Designer Pitfalls page identifies “a small amount of yarn” as a failure because a precise amount is missing, warns about discontinued colors, missing accessory quantities, one-size instructions, omitted repeats and counts, unexplained abbreviations, missing sides, missing descriptions, and schematics not drawn to scale. The same source separately distinguishes project math, gauge, instructions, graphics, contracts, and permission to change contracted work. This supports a film about qualifier loss without collapsing the underlying human craft gates. [2]

Knitty’s current June 17, 2026 submission guidance distinguishes pattern notes, unusual abbreviations, sizes, charts, schematics, images, rights/originality, technical editing, test knitting, and publication. A value such as gauge, size, material, or status therefore requires local meaning and limitations; a field cannot be treated as universally complete because it is populated. [3]

### 2. Plain-language guidance supports explicit conditions, but not a comprehension guarantee

The Department of the Interior’s current plain-language page states that plain language helps the public read, understand, and use government communications, and lists instructions, applications, technical reports, webpages, and notices as documents that should be clear and reliable. Digital.gov similarly frames plain language as writing, design, and testing for a specific audience. This supports showing qualifier words beside the value rather than burying them in a footnote. It does not establish that a particular audience will understand every qualifier or that Stitch & Scale automatically adds them. [4] [5]

### 3. Accessibility guidance treats labels and relationships as part of meaning

The W3C WCAG 2.1 Recommendation covers content on mobile and other devices and states that information, structure, and relationships conveyed through presentation must be available in text or programmatically determinable. It includes labels and instructions for user input, text alternatives for non-text content, captions for synchronized media, and cautions that even high conformance does not address every user need. For this concept, the qualifier must remain adjacent to its value in the visual and text transcript; color alone cannot carry `REQUIRED`, `ESTIMATED`, `UNKNOWN`, or `NOT APPLICABLE`. W3C does not prove product accessibility or interpretation capability. [6]

### 4. Seasonal and submission context

Current 2026 craft-publication calls and Knitty’s Winter 2026 context show that requirements vary by destination and stage. A field may be required for one submission, optional for another, estimated during planning, or unknown until a sample or editor confirms it. This supports destination- and stage-qualified wording, not urgency, affiliation, or a universal rule. No named event, publication, deadline, or partnership is included in the film. [3] [7] [8]

### 5. Platform, caption, audio, and mobile presentation

The film must use one narrator family, original or cleared audio, caption-on and caption-free reviews, and captions that keep the qualifier and its value together. Captions may not cover cards, values, units, numbers, faces, hands, tables, logos, controls, menus, or primary actions. W3C requires captions for prerecorded synchronized media; TikTok and YouTube provide caption workflows but automatic captions remain draft-only until human review. [9] [10] [11]

The visual opportunity is a single bare card reading `YARN: 400g` that gains a qualifier card: `ESTIMATE`, `FOR SIZE 4`, `PROJECT-SPECIFIC`, `SOURCE: UNCONFIRMED`. It then splits into `REQUIRED`, `OPTIONAL`, `UNKNOWN`, and `NOT APPLICABLE` examples. The film must not use actual Stitch & Scale labels, real pattern numbers, real customer data, or a fabricated product field.

## Uncertainty and limits

CYC and Knitty document craft workflow and destination-specific requirements, not Stitch & Scale functionality. A qualifier does not prove math, fit, gauge, chart readability, schematic scale, technical editing, test-knit success, accessibility, rights clearance, publication acceptance, or user outcome. Qualifier words can themselves be misunderstood and require audience, language, unit, size, stage, source, date, and human review. `ESTIMATE` is not a prediction guarantee; `UNKNOWN` is not proof that data are absent everywhere; `NOT APPLICABLE` requires a named scope; `REQUIRED` is destination- and stage-dependent; `OPTIONAL` does not mean useless. Automatic captions are draft-only. Rights for real pattern text, charts, schematics, screenshots, and records are unconfirmed.

No source supports claims about revenue, adoption, accuracy, customer outcomes, final pricing, credentials, testimonials, urgency, or unverified product capabilities. David is a developer whose late mother knitted; he must not be presented as a knitter, knitwear designer, pattern designer, technical editor, tester, publisher, accountant, lawyer, or tax adviser.

## Decision changed by research

Research changed the concept from a generic “label clarity” idea into the narrower **qualifier-state** angle. The film will show a bare value that looks authoritative, then attach a qualifier card with `STATUS`, `SCOPE`, `SOURCE`, `DATE`, and `LIMITATION`. It will show that required, optional, estimated, unknown, and not-applicable are not interchangeable. The emotional turn is from “the number is there” to “the number carries the conditions that make it usable.”

The proposed proof surface is `QUALIFIER Q-001 / FIELD / VALUE / QUALIFIER / SCOPE / UNIT / SIZE / STAGE / SOURCE / AS_OF / LIMITATION / HUMAN_CHECK / STATUS`. No current Stitch & Scale qualifier surface is verified.

The selected angle is materially different from pass 234: it is not about whether a source is editable or released, but about the semantic qualifier attached to one field or value. It does not reopen the imported `false-completion` or `release-gate-roster` reservations.
